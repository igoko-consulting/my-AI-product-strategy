Pitching to: CEO + Leadership Team

Trip Disruption Copilot

For leisure travellers, we turn the worst moment of a trip into the reason they book with us again, by fixing disruption before they know it has happened.

The Case · 1 of 3

Why now

Three things changed inside the last year.
Airline and hotel inventory APIs now let us hold a replacement seat or room before the carrier announces the cancellation, which is the difference between a fix and a notification. Watching every booked trip continuously now costs $1.80 per active trip-month, against the roughly $200 commission that trip already earns us, so the economics work at a scale that would have been prohibitive two years ago. And Google shipped trip parsing in Gmail, which tells us the alerting half of this is going to exist whether we build it or not.
The Case · 2 of 3

What's defensible

Not the alerting.
Google can take that, and we estimate roughly 40% of the value sits there. What they cannot take is execution: to actually move a booking, someone has to hold inventory, take payment and reissue the ticket, and that routes through us. Underneath it, every disruption we resolve adds to a cross-airline record of what actually works, which no model vendor has. We score that flywheel at 12 out of 20 today, which is honest rather than flattering: the personalisation and correction loops run, the network loop does not yet.
The Case · 3 of 3

The economics

$1.80 total cost per active trip-month against ~$200 of commission, at 91% gross margin, holding 58.5% through a correlated storm month when every affected traveller escalates at once.
The break-even is the number that matters: this pays for itself on a 0.9% lift in conversion or repeat booking. Cost is not the risk. We keep 94% of requests off the expensive model, and blended cost per request can rise 7.7x before margin reaches 40%.
The Risks

What could break this

Trust / Failure
The scenario that ends up in the press is not a missed alert. It is us cancelling a family's ticket for a flight that then departs normally. The architecture is built to make that impossible rather than unlikely: cancelling an original booking before its replacement is confirmed is never automatic at any confidence level, safety checks on spend and consent are gated at 100% rather than averaged into an accuracy score, and anything below 50% confidence goes to a human. One honest gap: our own product copy promises every action is reversible, and that stops being true once a ticket is reissued. We found it in a self-review, and fixing it is in the first four weeks.
Scale / Governance
Inference cost is not what breaks at 10x. Two things are. The human reviewer rota is currently carried at $0.10 per trip-month, which almost certainly understates a 24/7 operation, and a storm month sends the whole affected cohort to that queue simultaneously. Separately, automated rebooking is a decision with legal effect under GDPR, so a human intervention path is mandatory rather than optional, and we have not yet resolved whether charging an absent traveller's card satisfies European payment authentication rules. That last one could remove the fully automatic tier entirely.
Competitive
The scenario that forces a kill is not Google. It is carriers declining to let anyone hold inventory ahead of their own cancellation announcement, or pricing it punitively. That single unverified premise sits underneath the entire product. Kill criteria are set: if no carrier will permit a pre-announcement hold by week 4, we stop. If showing disruption risk at browse time does not shift booking selection by 3 percentage points by week 6, the browse-time half of the value is imagined and comes out.
